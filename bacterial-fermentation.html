Why Do Some Bacteria Ferment in the Presence of Oxygen?

Understanding aerobic fermentation and overflow metabolism in bacteria

Independent Literature Review
Raees · Biological Sciences · Imperial College London

1. Introduction

Fermentation is usually associated with conditions where oxygen is absent. In these conditions, many microorganisms use fermentation to keep producing energy when aerobic respiration cannot occur. However, some bacteria can produce fermentation products even when oxygen is available.

This may seem surprising because aerobic respiration usually produces more energy from glucose than fermentation. If oxygen is present, it might seem more useful for bacteria to use respiration instead.

However, bacteria such as Escherichia coli can produce organic acids such as acetate during rapid growth, even when oxygen is available. This is often called overflow metabolism. It happens when glucose enters the cell faster than the cell can process it through respiration.

This review explains why some bacteria ferment when oxygen is present and why this process can be useful during rapid growth.

2. Respiration and Fermentation

To understand this process, it is useful to compare respiration and fermentation.

During aerobic respiration, glucose is broken down in several stages. Glycolysis first breaks glucose into smaller molecules and produces some ATP. These products can then enter other pathways, such as the citric acid cycle, where more energy is produced.

During respiration, electrons are eventually passed to oxygen. This allows the cell to produce a relatively large amount of ATP from each glucose molecule.

Fermentation works differently. Instead of completely breaking down glucose through respiration, the cell changes some of its products into substances such as acetate, lactate or ethanol. This allows the cell to keep producing energy, but usually produces less ATP from each glucose molecule.

Fermentation does not only happen when oxygen is absent. Some bacteria can also use it when oxygen is present, depending on how quickly they are growing and how much glucose is available.

3. What Is Overflow Metabolism?

Overflow metabolism occurs when a cell produces and releases metabolic products even though oxygen is available.

In E. coli, a common example is the production of acetate during rapid growth on glucose. When there is a lot of glucose, E. coli can take it up very quickly. The glucose then enters glycolysis and other metabolic pathways.

However, the cell can only process a certain amount of glucose through respiration. If glucose enters the cell faster than the respiratory pathways can handle, some of the carbon is sent into other pathways instead.

This can lead to the production and release of acetate.

At first, this may seem wasteful because the cell gets less energy from glucose than it would through complete respiration. However, acetate is not always useless waste. The cell can use it later as a source of carbon and energy.

4. Why Can't the Bacterium Simply Respire More?

A simple question is why bacteria do not just use more respiration when there is plenty of glucose.

Respiration requires many proteins and enzymes. These take time and resources for the cell to produce.

When a bacterium is growing quickly, it needs to use its resources for many different processes, such as making proteins, copying DNA and producing energy.

Because of this, the cell has a limit to how much glucose it can process through respiration.

Fermentation can produce less energy from glucose, but it can allow the cell to process glucose quickly without needing as much respiratory machinery.

This can help explain why fermentation sometimes happens even when oxygen is available.

5. The Importance of Growth Rate

The growth rate of the bacterium is also important.

When E. coli grows slowly and there is not much glucose available, it can usually use respiration to process the glucose.

However, when there is a lot of glucose, E. coli can take it up very quickly. The cell may then receive glucose faster than its respiratory system can process it.

Some of the carbon is therefore redirected towards acetate production.

This means that oxygen is not the only factor controlling fermentation. Glucose availability and growth rate also matter.

6. Is Fermentation Wasteful?

Fermentation may seem wasteful because it produces less energy from glucose than respiration.

However, it can still be useful.

A rapidly growing bacterium may benefit from processing glucose quickly rather than getting the maximum amount of energy from every molecule.

This creates a trade-off:

Respiration → more energy per glucose molecule
Fermentation → faster processing with fewer resources

The bacterium can therefore use both processes depending on its conditions.

Acetate should also not always be considered waste. When glucose becomes limited, E. coli can take up acetate and use it as a source of carbon and energy.

7. What Happens to the Acetate?

The acetate produced by E. coli does not necessarily go to waste.

When glucose becomes limited, E. coli can take up acetate and use it as a source of carbon and energy.

This change in metabolism is sometimes called the acetate switch.

This means that acetate can be useful later, even though it was produced during rapid growth.

The process shows that overflow metabolism is not simply the disposal of unwanted products. It can be part of a larger strategy that helps bacteria respond to changes in their environment.

8. Why Might This Be Useful?

Bacteria often experience changing conditions.

When glucose is abundant, producing acetate can help the cell deal with the large amount of carbon entering its metabolism.

When glucose becomes limited, the bacterium can change its metabolism and use acetate or other nutrients instead.

This gives bacteria flexibility and allows them to respond to changes in their environment.

Overflow metabolism shows that bacteria must balance several factors, including energy production, growth rate and the resources needed to maintain their metabolism.

9. Conclusion

Some bacteria can produce fermentation products even when oxygen is available. In E. coli, the production of acetate during rapid growth on glucose is a well-studied example of overflow metabolism.

This happens when glucose enters the cell faster than the respiratory pathways can process it. Instead of sending all the glucose through respiration, the cell redirects some of it towards pathways that produce acetate.

Although fermentation produces less energy per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration, it can help rapidly growing bacteria process glucose quickly while using fewer resources.

This shows that the most energy-efficient pathway is not always the best option for a growing cell. Bacteria must balance energy production, growth rate and the resources needed to maintain their metabolism.

Acetate is also not always simply waste. When glucose becomes limited, bacteria can use acetate as a source of carbon and energy.

Overall, aerobic fermentation demonstrates the flexibility of bacterial metabolism. It shows that bacteria can use different metabolic pathways depending on the conditions around them, even when oxygen is present.
